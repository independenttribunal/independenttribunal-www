MVL / 2026 / No. 1 — Instrument of Onboarding

The New System.

The smallest set of laws that can work, built from scratch. The sad, serious business of keeping law honest.

We are not patching the old system. We are writing a new one from scratch — minimal by default, answerable to conscience, open to evidence. Then we hold it against the old one, with a mandate to improve it.

Article 1 — First principle

Life comes first

Before every other rule

Do not kill. Do not murder. Do not unjustifiably harm. Care for people, for other living creatures, and for the systems that sustain life.

Every article below exists to serve this one. A law that does not serve life fails its review. A court that punishes care has lost its way.

Article 2 — The ten points

Ten points each — and one sentence why

Root causes go unfixed because nobody has to state their reasons in public, side by side. So the new system opens a standing forum with one rule. Any organisation may enter, and every organisation enters the same way — ten points, one sentence of justification each.

Ten forces you to choose what matters most. One sentence forces you to say why — no burying it in a hundred-page report nobody reads. The sameness is the whole point. A protest group, a bank, a police force, an arms maker, a government department: same board, same shape. Their reasons sit side by side — and refusing to enter is itself visible.

Entry No. 1 — Ten Points The New System
  1. Life comes first. Because every other rule exists to serve it.
  2. The jury answers to conscience. Because a law the public refuses to enforce has lost its permission, and someone must be able to say so out loud.
  3. Justification is always heard. Because no court should convict someone it has forbidden to explain themselves.
  4. The right to prosecute is earned by evidence, not by office. Because a gate that decides in advance which cases are worth hearing decides who the law is for.
  5. Every act is recorded in separate fields. Because judgment fails when reason, body, mind and the view of others are tangled into one story.
  6. Every law expires unless renewed. Because a law that cannot justify itself again is obeyed out of habit, not consent.
  7. Thresholds are named and ordered. Because a careless mistake and a chosen, repeated failure are different things and deserve different consequences.
  8. Honest belief is not truth. Because a lie repeated often enough is believed sincerely, and sincerity does not make it true.
  9. Serious accusations carry time limits, with serious exceptions. Because evidence decays, and a defence that can no longer be mounted is not a trial.
  10. Terrorism means intent to kill or injure. Because a word that covers both mass murder and spray paint no longer tells you anything about right and wrong.
  11. Every organisation states its ten points and justifies each in one sentence. Because power that will not explain itself in public is not accountable in private.

A person must always be allowed to say why. An institution must always be required to. Justification said openly, not hidden.

Article 3 — The record

A standard record of what happened — and why

Judgment fails when everything gets tangled into one story. The record keeps things apart: three fields of testimony, then one multi-step test of judgment by others.

Form MVL-1 · Standard Record of an Act Specimen
1 · Justification — required, always

Why the person did it. There is always a reason. If there was none, "no reason" becomes the reason — and is recorded as such. No court can ever forbid this field, and it can never be left blank.

2 · Physical state — recorded by default

Drunk · on drugs · sick · exhausted · no sleep. Always a factor. A condition of the body, not a state of mind.

3 · State of mind — subjective

What the person was thinking in the moment. Their own testimony, kept strictly apart from the body's condition.

4 · Judgment by others — the multi-step test

Not one "reasonable person" but five distinct viewpoints, each answered on its own.

4a · Then

What others could fairly have thought in that moment, knowing only what was knowable then.

4b · Now

What others conclude today, knowing everything that has come to light since.

4c · By people like me

The judgment of peers — people in similar circumstances, who know what those circumstances demand.

4d · By a judge

The trained, dispassionate reading of the law — what lawyers call objective reasonableness.

4e · Any other considerations

Anything the first four steps could not hold. Named, not smuggled in.

When Field 1 keeps drawing sympathy under the same law, that law goes to review. The record and the jury are the same signal, written down.

Article 4 — Who may prosecute

The right to prosecute is earned by evidence, not office

Today, prosecution passes through gates. The main gate is the CPS — the Crown Prosecution Service, the state's prosecutor. It applies a two-stage test: is conviction realistic, and is prosecuting in the public interest.7 Anyone else can bring a private prosecution, but only if a magistrate approves it first. Even then, the CPS can take the case over and drop it.8

The new system removes the gates. Anyone who builds a dossier — a solid file of evidence — has the authority to prosecute. The case goes straight to court, where the dossier is tested in the open. The filter is the trial itself, not a door in front of it. Open prosecution in, conscience acquittal out — two valves, keeping the system honest in both directions.

Article 5 — The default

Every law expires unless renewed

Every law carries a review date. At review, the default outcome is expiry — renewal is earned, never inherited. A law that cannot justify itself again leaves the books. The old system has nothing like this, which is how each of these is still an offence:

  • Handling salmon in suspicious circumstancesSalmon Act 1986
  • Being drunk in a pubLicensing Act 1872
  • Wearing a suit of armour in ParliamentStatute of 1313
  • Carrying a plank along a pavementMetropolitan Police Act 1839

The joke is not the laws. The joke is that the system has no way to let them go.

Article 7 — Definitions

Thresholds, named and ordered

Judgment needs a ladder, not a blur. The new system names every rung:

Negligence
Falling short of the care the situation asked for.
Gross negligence
Falling so far short that the failure itself deserves blame.
Wilful gross negligence
Knowing the failure and choosing it anyway.
Persistent wilful gross negligence
Choosing it again, and again, after being told.

Truth needs the same ladder. A lie repeated often enough can be genuinely believed — and honest belief is not the same as truth. So statements are graded:

True and verified
Said, checked, and checkable by anyone else.
Honest belief, unverified
Genuinely believed, never checked — including beliefs planted by repetition.
Reckless assertion
Said without caring whether it is true.
Knowing falsehood
Said while knowing it is false.

Field 3 of the record captures what a person believed. This ladder records how that belief stands against the truth — two different questions, never merged.

Article 8 — Time

Reasonable limits, serious exceptions

England and Wales put no time limit on prosecuting serious crimes. Most of Europe sets limits even for serious offences — England and Wales are the outlier.13 For alleged sexual offences involving adults, the new system proposes a presumptive ten-year limit. It applies only where no complaint was made within that time. "Presumptive" means a normal rule the prosecution can overcome — a default, not a wall. The reasons are about evidence, not morals. Evidence decays, memories change with retelling, and a decades-old evening becomes impossible to defend fairly.

No clock ever
Crimes against children and minors, plus war crimes, genocide and crimes against humanity, never time out.
The clock pauses
Coercion, incapacity or deliberate cover-up pauses the clock — silence the perpetrator created never counts in their favour.
The past reopens
A new victim, or credible new evidence showing a pattern, reopens older linked allegations.
Always
Innocence is presumed; late complaints are still heard; courts weigh delay, evidence and fairness on the record.

Hear late complaints; protect fair trials. Not "believe" or "disbelieve" — keep the fields separate and judge each on the record.

Article 9 — The first case

The Independent Tribunal — the system, in use

A system is proved by what it is willing to try. IndependentTribunal.org is the first tool built on these rules. It gathers evidence against people who, from public office, enabled genocide, crimes against humanity or war crimes — and sends it to every authority that can act.

It starts from findings already on the record:

  • UN Commission of Inquiry, Sept 2025: genocide has been committed in Gaza.15
  • International Criminal Court, Nov 2024: arrest warrants for crimes against humanity and starvation as a weapon.16
  • Al-Haq and GLAN: UK arms export licences taken to the High Court and Court of Appeal.17

The old system indicts itself To prosecute war crimes in England and Wales you need the Attorney General's consent.18 A political officer holds the gate in front of prosecuting political officers. Article 4 was written for exactly this door. Proposal: replace that consent with a judge's decision, in open court, with reasons.

The standard: name the conduct, cite the sources, allege a case to answer — never declare guilt a court has not. Same standard for every side.

Article 10 — The jury

The jury answers to conscience

If a case is grossly unfair — immoral, obscene — the jury can say no. That is the foundation under everything else. Since Bushel's Case in 1670, no juror can be punished for their verdict.1 Ponting walked free in 1985, against the judge's direction.2 The Colston Four walked in 2022, after Rajiv Menon KC told the jury it could acquit on conscience.3

The old system punishes people for saying this out loud. David Nixon of Insulate Britain was jailed eight weeks for contempt — for telling the jury why he did it.4 Giovanna Lewis and Amy Pritchard were jailed seven weeks for mentioning the climate crisis.5 Trudi Warner was prosecuted over a sign about jury conscience; the High Court threw the case out.6

Now it reaches the lawyers too. In January 2026, Rajiv Menon KC gave a closing speech invoking jury conscience, defending one of the Filton accused. For that speech he faces contempt proceedings — up to two years in prison. He is the first barrister in modern British legal history prosecuted for a closing speech.14 A system that jails the defendant, the bystander, and now the barrister is not defending the trial. It is defending itself from the trial.

The new system says this power out loud. When juries keep acquitting on conscience under one law, that law goes to review — the public has withdrawn its mandate.

Article 11 — The word

A label that covers everything describes nothing

⚠ Disclaimer Politically sensitive. Toxic. Radioactive. Do not mention this article if you ever want to get a job.

At one end, "terrorism" means deliberately killing many civilians. At the other, it now covers broken windows at a factory and paint on a bank. A word doing both jobs no longer tells you anything about right and wrong. It becomes a lever, not a description — and a lever can be pulled at anyone.

This is not a fringe view. In July 2025, UN special rapporteurs — independent rights experts — issued a warning. Property damage without intent to kill or injure, they said, is not terrorism. They called the UK out of step with comparable democracies.11 In February 2026 the High Court ruled a protest group's proscription — its official ban as terrorist — unlawful. The Court of Appeal reversed that in June 2026.12 When senior judges split on whether a ban was lawful, it is a live question, not settled fact.

The new system's rule: terrorism means intent to kill or injure people. Break a window, and you answer for a broken window — at the scale of the damage done. A terrorist ban is a law like any other: justified in public, tested in open court. And it expires unless renewed.

Once you have seen this label cover a broken window, look again at everyone it has ever covered. In both directions, without flinching at what you find.
